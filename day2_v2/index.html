Question 2 · A column or a whole dataset?

Explain the difference between these commands. Which one extracts the response measurements? What do the two numbers from dim() represent?

soil_data$WSA
dim(soil_data)
Reveal answer: columns and dimensions

$WSA extracts the WSA column as a vector. dim(soil_data) gives the number of rows, then the number of columns. For the supplied file these are 353 and 23. A row records a soil experimental unit; different measurements in its columns do not create extra independent replicates.

Question 3 · Which rows are selected?

Read this command in words. What does == mean, and why is there a comma before the closing bracket? Would soil_data$D == 1 select the same rows?

drought_data <- soil_data[soil_data$remark == "D", ]
Reveal answer: selecting drought alone

Keep the rows whose treatment label equals "D", keep all columns, and store the result as drought_data. In [rows, columns], the empty position after the comma means “all columns.” == compares values; <- assigns an object.

remark == "D" selects drought alone. The indicator D == 1 also selects combinations containing drought and other factors. That would change our ecological comparison.

Question 4 · Is a missing observation a zero?

Predict both results. How many observed values support the second calculation?

practice <- c(0.50, NA, 0.70)
mean(practice)
mean(practice, na.rm = TRUE)
Reveal answer: missing values

The first result is NA. The second is 0.60, calculated from two observed values. na.rm = TRUE excludes missing values from that calculation; it does not replace them with zero or recover the missing measurements.

Question 5 · Install or load?

Which command installs a package on your computer, and which loads it in a new R session? Must you install it every time?

Reveal answer: packages

install.packages() installs the package and normally needs an internet connection. library() loads an installed package into the current session. You normally install once for your R installation and load it in each fresh session where you need it.

1. Connect our question to the experiment

Purpose: decide what we are comparing before running a test.

Our question continues from Day 1:

How much does mean water-stable aggregate proportion differ between drought and control soil, and how uncertain is that estimate?

WSA is the proportion of water-stable aggregates, recorded from 0 to 1. A value of 0.60 corresponds to 60%. We will keep the contrast in the same direction throughout:

Mean difference = mean under drought − mean under control.

Do together: identify the response, treatment groups, experimental unit, and role of the control. Explain why several measurements from one experimental unit would not automatically be independent replicates. Independence comes from the experimental design; an R command cannot establish it.

Write your ecological prediction and a possible reason as comments in your script. For our planned two-sided test:

  • H₀: the population mean WSA is the same in the two groups.
  • H₁: the population means differ.

A directional ecological prediction can accompany this two-sided test. We keep the two-sided choice for this exercise rather than selecting a direction after seeing the results. Today is a guided learning analysis of data you have already explored.

3. Estimate the size of the difference

Purpose: give the ecological difference a direction, a magnitude, and units.

mean_control <- mean(control_WSA)
mean_drought <- mean(drought_WSA)
mean_difference <- mean_drought - mean_control

mean_control
mean_drought
mean_difference
100 * mean_difference

The observed mean difference is an effect-size estimate. We can calculate it directly from the data. A confidence interval, which we will obtain next, adds information about its uncertainty.

Do: write a sentence explaining the sign and size of your result. What would a positive difference mean? Why did we multiply by 100 in the last command?

Check: proportion units and percentage points

The means are approximately 0.606 for control and 0.462 for drought. Drought minus control is −0.144 in proportion units, equivalent to −14.4 percentage points. This describes a lower average under drought in the supplied data.

Multiplying a proportion difference by 100 expresses it in percentage points. A relative percentage change would be a different calculation with a denominator. Always name your units.

Question for discussion: is a difference of this size ecologically important? Explain what additional knowledge about soil functioning or measurement would help you judge that. Statistical significance alone will not answer it.

4. Consider the design and inspect the groups

Purpose: understand why a method may be appropriate for the question and data.

Today we use Welch’s independent two-sample t-test to compare population means. It does not require the two group variances or sample sizes to be equal. Its inference still depends on an appropriate design and distributional assumptions; it does not solve dependence, strong skew, or extreme observations.

Use the figure and these summaries to discuss:

length(control_WSA)
length(drought_WSA)
summary(control_WSA)
summary(drought_WSA)
  1. Are these measurements from separate experimental units? Are the groups paired, or independent? Check the experimental design with your instructor.
  2. Do any observations look especially unusual, or do the groups look strongly skewed?
  3. Does the small drought sample leave some distributional features difficult to assess?

Do: write one sentence about the design and one about the data inspection. Discuss concerns with the instructor before interpreting the test. An unusual observation should prompt investigation, not automatic deletion.

Optional instructor-guided check: Q–Q plots

A Q–Q plot compares the pattern of ordered observations with a normal distribution. Approximately straight patterns are consistent with approximate normality; small samples make the assessment uncertain. Use this supplied template if your instructor discusses it.

par(mfrow = c(1, 2))
qqnorm(control_WSA, main = "Control")
qqline(control_WSA)
qqnorm(drought_WSA, main = "Drought")
qqline(drought_WSA)
par(mfrow = c(1, 1))

Neither a visual plot nor a non-significant normality test proves normality. We are not using a Shapiro–Wilk p-value as an automatic switch between statistical tests. If the method is unsuitable, discuss an alternative that matches the design and scientific question.

5. Estimate uncertainty with a confidence interval

Purpose: place the estimated mean difference alongside its sampling uncertainty.

Run this one test. Drought comes first, so the interval is for drought minus control, matching our effect-size calculation.

test_result <- t.test(drought_WSA, control_WSA,
                      alternative = "two.sided",
                      var.equal = FALSE,
                      conf.level = 0.95)
test_result

For now, find the two group means and the 95% confidence interval in the output. You can return to the test statistic and degrees of freedom later.

test_result$estimate
test_result$conf.int

The group means in $estimate are labelled “mean of x” and “mean of y”: here x is drought and y is control. The confidence interval gives a range of mean differences compatible with the data under the statistical model, and its width indicates precision.

Do: write the interval limits and answer: are both limits negative, both positive, or on opposite sides of zero? Does the interval refer to individual soil measurements or to a difference between population means?

Check: reading this confidence interval

The 95% interval is approximately −0.238 to −0.051 in proportion units, or −23.8 to −5.1 percentage points. Both limits are negative, and zero is outside the interval. It estimates a difference between population means, not the range containing 95% of individual observations.

The 95% level describes the method’s long-run coverage: under its assumptions, about 95% of intervals made this way in repeated studies would cover the true difference. It is not a guarantee that this particular interval contains the true value, and it does not account for every possible source of bias.

Discuss these three examples. A is our course result; B and C are invented teaching examples. All differences have the same WSA proportion units.

Example Estimated difference 95% confidence interval
A · course result −0.144 −0.238 to −0.051
B · illustrative −0.144 −0.360 to 0.072
C · illustrative −0.010 −0.015 to −0.005

Which interval includes zero? Which is narrowest? Can two studies have the same estimated difference but different precision? Does an interval excluding zero automatically imply an ecologically important difference?

Reveal the interval discussion

B includes zero and is less precise than A despite having the same point estimate. C has the narrowest interval but a small estimated difference. An interval excluding zero can indicate a well-resolved difference under the model without establishing its ecological importance. An interval including zero does not prove that there is no effect.

6. Interpret the p-value and answer the question

Purpose: combine statistical evidence, effect size, and ecological interpretation.

test_result$p.value

For this test, the p-value is the probability of a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one, assuming equal population means and the other test assumptions. It is not the probability that H₀ is true, the probability your conclusion is wrong, or the size of the effect.

For this exercise we use the conventional decision threshold alpha = 0.05. When p < 0.05, we reject H₀ under the test assumptions. When p >= 0.05, we fail to reject it; this does not establish equality. Interpret the estimate and interval alongside that decision.

Do: write three short sentences using your own output:

  1. State which group had the higher mean and by how much, with units.
  2. Report the confidence interval and p-value, naming the test.
  3. Relate the result to your ecological prediction and state one limitation.
Check the numerical result and a possible report

The p-value is approximately 0.007. A possible report is:

Mean WSA was lower under drought than in the control group by 0.144 proportion units (14.4 percentage points). The Welch two-sample test gave a 95% confidence interval for drought minus control of −0.238 to −0.051, with p = 0.007. These observations support a lower mean WSA under the drought treatment in this experimental setting; they do not alone identify the mechanism or establish the same response in all soils.

Add your own ecological reasoning. The drought group has only seven observations, so inspection and uncertainty remain relevant even with a p-value below 0.05.

Correct these statements together:

  • “p = 0.007 means there is a 0.7% probability that the null hypothesis is true.”
  • “A non-significant result would prove drought has no effect.”
  • “A small p-value means the effect must be large and ecologically important.”
Reveal the corrections

The p-value is calculated under H₀ and the test assumptions; it does not assign H₀ a probability. Failure to reject H₀ can reflect uncertainty and is not proof of no effect. Ecological importance requires the estimated magnitude, its uncertainty, and knowledge of the system, not the p-value alone.

Optional preview: what does bootstrapping do?

For students who finish early or an instructor demonstration. The required analysis is complete. You do not need to write a loop or function today.

Bootstrapping uses repeated samples drawn with replacement from the observed data to approximate sampling uncertainty. For this two-group example, we resample within each group and calculate drought minus control each time. This keeps the original group sizes and the chosen contrast.

Show the supplied bootstrap demonstration

Download the optional demonstration script into your project folder, then use this supplied function after running the main workflow:

source("Day2_bootstrap_demo.R")
boot_result <- bootstrap_difference(drought_WSA, control_WSA,
                                    n_resamples = 4000, seed = 3)
boot_result$estimate
boot_result$conf.int
hist(boot_result$differences,
     xlab = "Drought minus control (WSA proportion)",
     main = "Bootstrap mean differences")

The solid line marks the observed difference; the dashed line marks zero. More bootstrap resamples reduce simulation noise, but they do not create more independent soil observations. Resampling from only seven drought observations still has limitations. A bootstrap interval need not equal the Welch interval, and a bootstrap tail area is not generally a t-test p-value. We will revisit resampling in the later null-model lesson.
